How PostgreSQL reads a query (keep this in mind)

You write SELECT first, but the database actually works in this order:

FROM / JOIN→WHERE→GROUP BY→HAVING→SELECT→ORDER BY→LIMIT

Once this order clicks, half the confusing errors stop making sense as errors and start making sense as logic.

一
Filtering rows

AND, OR (and NOT)

Before sorting or grouping, you first need to pick the right rows. That is the job of WHERE, and AND / OR let you put more than one condition in it.

Think of a mother sending you to the market:

  • AND = "Get tomatoes and onions." You come back only when both are done. Every condition must be true.
  • OR = "Get Amul or Mother Dairy milk." Any one is fine. At least one condition must be true.
  • NOT = "Anything except brinjal." It flips the condition.
Golden rule: SQL checks AND before OR, just like maths does × before +. When you mix both, always use brackets ( ) so SQL does exactly what you mean. Example 3 shows what goes wrong without them.

Example 1AND: Gurugram students who study MERN

Both conditions must be true. A Gurugram student in Python is rejected, and a Delhi student in MERN is also rejected.

-- Show these columns
SELECT name, city, course
-- from the students table
FROM students
-- condition 1: student must be from Gurugram
WHERE city = 'Gurugram'
-- condition 2: AND the course must be MERN (both must be true)
  AND course = 'MERN';

Example 3The bracket trap (very common mistake)

Goal: MERN students who are from Delhi or Noida. The first query looks right but it isn't. Since AND runs first, SQL reads it as (MERN AND Delhi) OR (Noida). So every Noida student comes in, even Python and Data Analytics ones.

-- WRONG version (no brackets)
SELECT name, city, course
FROM students
-- SQL reads this as: (course = 'MERN' AND city = 'Delhi') OR city = 'Noida'
WHERE course = 'MERN' AND city = 'Delhi' OR city = 'Noida';

Now the correct version. The brackets force SQL to first decide "Delhi or Noida", and only then check MERN.

-- RIGHT version (with brackets)
SELECT name, city, course
FROM students
-- first the course must be MERN
WHERE course = 'MERN'
-- and the city must be one of these two (brackets are solved first)
  AND (city = 'Delhi' OR city = 'Noida');

Example 4NOT, IN and BETWEEN: shortcuts for long AND/OR chains

IN is a short way of writing many ORs. BETWEEN is a short way of writing >= AND <=. NOT flips the result. Here: students not from Gurugram, with marks from 70 to 90 (both ends included).

-- Show these columns
SELECT name, city, marks
FROM students
-- NOT IN = city is none of these (same as city <> 'Gurugram')
WHERE city NOT IN ('Gurugram')
-- BETWEEN 70 AND 90 means marks >= 70 AND marks <= 90
  AND marks BETWEEN 70 AND 90;

二
Sorting

ORDER BY

Whenever a school puts up a merit list, the topper's name is at the top. Your attendance register goes from A to Z. That arranging is exactly what ORDER BY does.

  • ASC = small to big, A to Z, old date to new date. This is the default, so you can skip writing it.
  • DESC = big to small, Z to A, newest date first.
  • You can sort by more than one column. The second column is used only when the first one is a tie.
  • LIMIT n keeps only the first n rows after sorting. Perfect for "Top 5".
Remember: Without ORDER BY, the database gives no promise about the order of rows. If order matters to you, always write it.

Example 1Sort students by name, A to Z

Like an attendance register. We didn't write ASC because it is the default.

-- Show name and city
SELECT name, city
FROM students
-- arrange rows by name, A to Z (ASC is default)
ORDER BY name;

三
Making groups

GROUP BY

Imagine a teacher dumps all 15 admission forms on the table and says, "Make separate piles city-wise." Now you have a Gurugram pile, a Delhi pile and so on. Then you count each pile. That is GROUP BY.

After making groups, you use aggregate functions to get one answer per group:

  • COUNT(*) = how many rows in the group
  • SUM(col) = total
  • AVG(col) = average
  • MIN(col) / MAX(col) = smallest / biggest
The one rule beginners always break: every column in SELECT must either be in GROUP BY or be inside an aggregate like COUNT or SUM. If you group by city and also select name, PostgreSQL will throw an error, because one city pile has many names and it doesn't know which one to show.

Example 1How many students from each city?

One pile per city, then count each pile. 15 rows become 4 rows.

-- show the city and the count of students in it
SELECT city, COUNT(*) AS total_students
FROM students
-- make one group (pile) per city
GROUP BY city
-- biggest pile first
ORDER BY total_students DESC;
Output · 4 rows
citytotal_students
Gurugram6

Noida3
Faridabad2

Example 3Average marks per course (and how NULL behaves)

COUNT(*) counts every row, but COUNT(marks) skips NULLs. AVG also skips NULLs. So in MERN there are 6 students but only 5 marks, and the average is of those 5. ROUND(..., 1) keeps one decimal place.

SELECT course,
       -- all students in the course
       COUNT(*)              AS students,
       -- only students who have marks (NULL is skipped)
       COUNT(marks)          AS students_with_marks,
       -- average of available marks, 1 decimal place
       ROUND(AVG(marks), 1)  AS avg_marks
FROM students
-- one group per course
GROUP BY course
ORDER BY avg_marks DESC;

Filtering groups

HAVING

Once you have made the city-wise piles, the principal says, "Only show me the cities that have more than 3 students." You can't use WHERE for this, because WHERE looks at one form at a time, before the piles are even made. It has no idea how big a pile is.

HAVING is the filter for piles. It runs after GROUP BY.

WHEREHAVING
Filterssingle rowswhole groups
Runsbefore GROUP BYafter GROUP BY
Can use COUNT, SUM?NoYes
Order in which PostgreSQL actually runs a query:
FROM → WHERE → GROUP BY → HAVING → SELECT → ORDER BY → LIMIT
That is why you cannot use a column nickname made in SELECT inside HAVING in PostgreSQL. Write the full expression again, like HAVING COUNT(*) > 3.

Example 1Cities with more than 3 students

First we make city piles, then HAVING throws away piles of size 3 or less.

SELECT city, COUNT(*) AS total_students
FROM students
-- make city-wise groups
GROUP BY city
-- keep only groups that have more than 3 students
HAVING COUNT(*) > 3;
Output · 2 rows
citytotal_students
Gurugram6
Delhi4

Example 2Courses that collected more than ₹80,000

SUM is calculated per course, and HAVING keeps only the big earners.

SELECT course, SUM(fee_paid) AS total_fee
FROM students
GROUP BY course
-- keep only courses whose total fee is above 80,000
HAVING SUM(fee_paid) > 80000
ORDER BY total_fee DESC;
Output · 2 rows
coursetotal_fee
MERN109000
Data Analytics88000

Example 3WHERE and HAVING together

Question: which cities have at least 2 students who paid ₹15,000 or more? WHERE first removes the low-fee students row by row. Then groups are made from what is left. Then HAVING checks the size of each group.

SELECT city, COUNT(*) AS students_paid_15k_plus
FROM students
-- step 1 (row filter): keep only students who paid 15,000 or more
WHERE fee_paid >= 15000
-- step 2: make city groups from the remaining rows
GROUP BY city
-- step 3 (group filter): keep cities with at least 2 such students
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 2
ORDER BY students_paid_15k_plus DESC;
Output · 3 rows
citystudents_paid_15k_plus
Gurugram4
Delhi4
Noida2

Example 4Courses where average marks are above 70

HAVING works with AVG too. Python drops out because its average is low.

SELECT course, ROUND(AVG(marks), 1) AS avg_marks
FROM students
GROUP BY course
-- keep courses whose average marks are above 70
HAVING AVG(marks) > 70;

Every row with every row

CROSS JOIN

A shop has 3 T-shirt designs and 4 colours. How many different T-shirts can it make? 3 × 4 = 12. Every design is paired with every colour. That is a CROSS JOIN.

It joins each row of table A with each row of table B. There is no ON condition because nothing is being matched. Everything is paired with everything.

Rows in result = rows in A × rows in B. Our 3 courses × 3 time slots = 9 rows.

Careful: 10,000 rows × 10,000 rows = 10 crore rows. Use CROSS JOIN only on small tables, or with a one-row table (like an average), as you will see in Example 3.

Example 1Every course in every time slot

3 courses × 3 slots = 9 possible batches. Great for planning a timetable.

SELECT c.course_name, t.slot_name, t.timing
-- c is a short nickname (alias) for courses
FROM courses c
-- pair every course with every time slot
CROSS JOIN time_slots t
-- arrange neatly
ORDER BY c.course_name, t.slot_name;

Example 3Compare every student with the class average

This is the most useful real-life trick. The inner query gives just ONE row (the average). Cross joining with one row simply sticks that value next to every student, so you can compare.

SELECT s.name,
       s.marks,
       -- the overall average, same value on every row
       a.avg_marks,
       -- how far above (+) or below (-) the average
       s.marks - a.avg_marks AS difference
FROM students s
-- a one-row table that holds the class average
CROSS JOIN (SELECT ROUND(AVG(marks), 1) AS avg_marks FROM students) a
-- skip students who haven't given the test
WHERE s.marks IS NOT NULL
ORDER BY difference DESC;

A table joined with itself

SELF JOIN

Look at the staff table. Meena's row says manager_id = 1. But who is 1? To find out, you have to look into the same table again and find staff_id 1. That is Rakesh.

A self join is just a normal join where both sides are the same table. The trick is to give the table two nicknames, as if you printed it twice:

  • e = the employee copy
  • m = the manager copy

Then you match e.manager_id = m.staff_id. There is no special keyword called SELF JOIN. You write JOIN or LEFT JOIN as usual.

Where you'll see this in real life: employee and manager, a student and the friend who referred them, a category and its parent category, or finding pairs (two students from the same city).

Example 1Every staff member with their manager's name

Read it as: take an employee from copy e, then find their boss in copy m. Notice Rakesh (Director) is missing. He has no manager, so INNER JOIN finds no match and drops him.

SELECT e.name AS employee,
       e.role,
       -- name taken from the manager copy
       m.name AS manager
-- first copy of the table, nickname e (employee)
FROM staff e
-- second copy of the same table, nickname m (manager)
JOIN staff m
  -- employee's manager_id must match the manager's staff_id
  ON e.manager_id = m.staff_id
ORDER BY e.staff_id;

Example 4Top referrers (self join + GROUP BY + HAVING)

Now we mix everything you've learned. Group the matches by referrer and keep people who brought 2 or more students. These are the students you should thank or reward.

SELECT r.name AS referrer,
       -- how many students this person brought
       COUNT(*) AS students_referred
FROM students s
JOIN students r
  ON s.referred_by = r.student_id
-- one group per referrer
GROUP BY r.name
-- only those who referred at least 2
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 2
ORDER BY students_referred DESC, r.name;
